MODEL & METHODS

From a triangle mesh to a force estimate.

The simulator splits your surface into triangles, rotates their outward normals into the airflow, and adds up their estimated pressure and friction forces.

01 / AIR CONDITIONS

Dynamic pressure

q=12ρV2

Dynamic pressure sets the force scale. Doubling airspeed multiplies it by four; doubling density multiplies it by two.

q
Dynamic pressure, Pa
ρ
Air density, kg/m³
V
Airspeed, m/s
02 / SURFACE EXPOSURE

Pressure on each triangle

Cp,i=2[max(0,−ni·u)]2

A triangle facing into the wind receives the largest modeled pressure coefficient. A triangle facing away receives zero pressure in this approximation. The pressure colors show this coefficient, from 0 to 2.

nᵢ
Outward unit normal of triangle i
u
Unit airflow direction, (+1, 0, 0)
03 / ADD THE FORCES

Pressure and skin friction

Fp,i=−qCp,iAini
Ff,i=qCfAi[u−(ni·u)ni]
F=∑i(Fp,i+Ff,i)

Pressure acts inward, opposite the outward normal. Friction follows the airflow projected onto each triangle’s tangent plane. Triangle area Aᵢ is measured in m²; the fixed friction coefficient Cf is 0.006.

Drag is the +X force component, vertical lift is +Y, and side force is +Z. Forces are reported in newtons.

04 / COMPARE RUNS

Coefficients and flow scales

CD=DqSCL=LqS
Re=ρVℓμM=Va

S is the unrotated X–Z bounding rectangle of your model, held fixed during rotation. ℓ is its physical X length. Viscosity μ is fixed at 1.81 × 10−5 Pa·s and sound speed a at 343 m/s.

Re and Mach provide context; they do not change this solver’s pressure or friction coefficients. At zero airspeed, coefficients display as zero by convention.

What these results mean

This is an educational surface-force approximation, not a CFD solution. It does not resolve circulation, suction, stall, separation, turbulence, compressibility, or shielding between surfaces. In particular, it cannot predict accurate wing lift or stall angles. Concave, overlapping, or internal surfaces may be overcounted. Use closed meshes with consistently outward-facing normals.

Particles illustrate airflow direction; they are not a solved velocity field. The coefficient equations normalize forces but do not validate the triangle-pressure approximation.

LEARN BY EXPERIMENTING

Change one thing. Follow the result.

These guided experiments use presets so you can reproduce the result. Experiment buttons start a new run and replace the current test article; export any data you want to keep first.

LESSON 01 / AIRSPEED

Why speed matters so much

Test the same sphere at 20 and 40 m/s. Keep its size, orientation, and density fixed. Because force scales with V² here, drag at 40 m/s is four times its value at 20 m/s.

What to watch

Dynamic pressure and drag increase fourfold. The drag coefficient stays the same because the speed factor cancels in D/(qS). Reynolds and Mach increase, but this model does not use them to alter the coefficients.

LESSON 02 / ORIENTATION

Give the wind a different surface

Start with the wing at zero pitch, then rotate it to 15°. Use Side view to inspect the angle. Pitch changes the normal of every triangle and therefore its exposure to the wind.

How to read the graphs

The history graph follows your run through time. The pitch sweep predicts −30° to +30° at the current airspeed, yaw, and roll. Lift is signed: a negative value acts downward. These curves do not include circulation or stall, so they are not real airfoil performance curves.

LESSON 03 / PHYSICAL SCALE

A bigger model catches more air

Compare a sphere with a 1 m diameter to one with a 2 m diameter, at 30 m/s. Doubling every linear dimension makes each triangle’s area four times larger, giving four times the modeled force.

Scale versus camera zoom

Changing Model length changes physical dimensions, surface area, force, and Reynolds number. Camera zoom and view buttons only change what you see. Drag coefficient remains unchanged under uniform scaling in this solver because both drag and reference area grow by the same factor.

LESSON 04 / SHAPE

Compare a curved and a flat front

Compare a sphere and a cube with the same 2 m X length. Their surfaces distribute the wind-facing normals differently. Watch the pressure colors and drag change.

Make a fair comparison

Both articles use the same bounding reference area, airspeed, density, and orientation in this experiment. Keep those fixed when comparing coefficients. The model ignores wake drag and separation, so the predicted difference is a lesson in the approximation, not a validated real-world ranking.

LESSON 05 / YOUR OWN MODEL

Prepare a useful test article

  1. Export a closed mesh as STL, OBJ, or self-contained GLB.
  2. Align its length with X, vertical direction with Y, and span with Z.
  3. Upload it and enter its real X length in metres.
  4. Use Side, Front, and Top to check the axes, then vary one setting at a time.
  5. Export the CSV to compare conditions and forces.

Reversed normals can reverse pressure forces. Internal and intersecting surfaces can add forces that real airflow would never reach. Simplify your mesh and inspect the export before drawing conclusions.

LESSON 06 / THE BIGGER PICTURE

From this workbench to real aerodynamics

Real flow can accelerate over a wing, create suction, circulate, separate, and form wakes. Resolving those effects requires a more complete physical model and validation against experiments.

What would a CFD solver add?

A CFD solver discretizes the surrounding fluid volume, solves conservation of mass and momentum, applies boundary conditions, and may model turbulence. Mesh quality, convergence, and validation still matter. This workbench sums independent surface estimates; increasing its triangle count does not turn it into CFD.

What can I learn reliably here?

You can explore the implemented relationships: speed-squared scaling, area scaling, normal projection, coordinate systems, coefficient normalization, and controlled comparisons. Treat absolute forces and detailed shape performance as illustrative.
